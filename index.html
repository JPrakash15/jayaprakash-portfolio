Full write-up: brief, approach & system

The brief

HelpFlow AI needed to position itself as an AI-powered customer support platform - dense enough to prove technical credibility, simple enough for a founder evaluating five tools in one sitting to get it in seconds.

Approach

Warm didn't mean warm colours. Blue is the professional foundation - the same trust signal Intercom, Zendesk, and Freshdesk anchor on - with a single purple-to-blue gradient accent used in exactly two moments: the closing CTA and the hero's AI-thinking line. Geist carries the technical-credibility read at every size.

What shipped

  • A conversion-first homepage with a clear single CTA hierarchy
  • A componentized design system (buttons, cards, forms, nav) built for handoff, with dev-ready layer naming
  • Responsive behaviour specified down to mobile breakpoints

Outcome

Delivered as a live, browsable prototype rather than static frames - so reviewers could evaluate real interaction and flow, not just the visual layer.

Full write-up: problem, approach & four platforms

The problem

Soullylush's customer-facing website, its shopping app, and the internal dashboards store managers and farmers relied on had each grown on their own terms - different visual language, different interaction logic, no shared sense that they belonged to the same brand.

Approach across four surfaces

Same colour system, same typography roles, same component logic - adapted to radically different stakes each time. The web homepage leads with brand storytelling; the app assumes higher intent and optimises for task completion; Soullyland trades imagery for stat cards and a live field-risk map; the BI dashboard reuses that same Overview grid so a manager never has to relearn a layout, only a vocabulary.

Tradeoffs made on purpose

  • Certifications placed below the hero, not above the fold - let value land first, trust second
  • A checkbox cart mapped to the physical habit of ticking off a grocery list
  • Two of five mobile nav slots spent on Reorder and Track - retention over browsing convenience
  • The BI dashboard kept separate from the farmer dashboard - same system, different data literacy

Outcome

A 14-section design rationale document ties every major decision back to a user or business reason, and named success metrics turn each tradeoff above into something testable, not permanent.

Full write-up: research, competitors & IA

The problem

Tamil Nadu's tourism offering spans heritage temples, hill stations, coastline, and festivals - but the existing site presented it as one long, undifferentiated list, asking every visitor to do the sorting themselves.

Approach

Benchmarking Kerala Tourism, Incredible India, and MakeMyTrip exposed the gap: shallow theming, weak booking trust, almost no traveler voice. Two personas - a mobile-first IT professional planning family trips, a traveler chasing hidden destinations - and twelve user stories grounded every hypothesis that followed.

What this covered

  • Competitor & benchmark review against other state tourism boards
  • Visitor-intent mapping and a revised information architecture
  • A redesigned homepage and district/destination templates
  • Real traveler stories and ratings surfaced on the homepage instead of buried in a blog

Outcome

A clearer path from "I'm curious about Tamil Nadu" to "here's my three-day itinerary," with the visual identity carrying the state's heritage without slipping into tourist-brochure cliché.

FAQ

How I work

Everything you'd normally ask before reaching out.

01 How I Approach & Solve problems?
I involve users from the initial stage, then diagnose the real issue - cognitive load, a skill gap, or a structural factor - before deciding how to solve it. From there I break the problem into chunks, test and validate each one for sufficiency (not minimalism), and check that no chunk's fix creates friction elsewhere before shipping.
02 How do I ensure my designs are user-centered?
I involve users from the initial stage of problem-solving, not just for validation at the end. That way the product is shaped by actual user needs and behavior from the start, rather than generalized assumptions about who the user is.
03 What is my design process?
I scale my process to the project, not the reverse. Lean UX for low-risk work. Double Diamond or Design Thinking for medium complexity. My full diagnostic process -> chunking, sequencing, interference-checks -> for high-stakes, multi-audience problems. The rigor matches what the problem demands, not a fixed ritual.
04 How I measure the success of a product?
I measure a product's success through KPIs - conversion rate, task completion rate, user retention - combined with usability testing reports and direct user feedback. But I keep in mind that people don't always do what they say, so I never rely on stated feedback alone. I cross-check it against actual behavior - what users complete, drop off from, or return to - because that gap between what people say and what they do is often where the real insight is. Ultimately, a product is only successful if it aligns with business goals while delivering a seamless user experience - one without the other isn't success.
05 How do I know when a design is "done"?
A design is done when each part of it resolves the specific problem it was meant to solve - not when it looks finished. If a chunk still shows friction for a user or doesn't move the metric it was meant to move, it's not done, regardless of visual polish.
06 How I validate the right problem to solve in a product?
Validating the right problem means looking past the UI. A product's real issue can live in taxonomy or UX writing, in a broken user flow, or in a mismatch between the product and how actual users behave - not just how it looks. I validate this through reports and behavioral data - task completion, drop-offs, conversion patterns - mapped against what users are actually doing, not just what they say. If a product fails to convert, that's rarely a visual problem; it's usually a sign the flow doesn't match real user behavior, and that's where I look first before touching any screen.

About

Design should move people, not just impress them.

I'm a Chennai-based product and UX designer, working across client projects, while mentoring design students at Image Creative Education - using their real briefs as the teaching ground.

"A design that only looks strong hasn't done its job yet - colour, type, and structure exist to move someone toward a decision."

I rarely hand students a solution. I ask the question that gets them to find it themselves - because that's the only way the thinking actually sticks. It's the same instinct I bring to my own work: a SaaS onboarding flow, a farmer's wellness dashboard, a state tourism platform - different problems, same underlying question: what does this person need to feel and understand to take the next step?
